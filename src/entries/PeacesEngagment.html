Today we have a guest post from Mom.

On Saturday, we went to an engagement party for Peace, Mother Thandie’s daughter. Peace was Mtemba’s teacher when we were doing the paperwork to adopt him. She taught English to the kids who were getting adopted.

The party was at Thandie’s house. We arrived around 10:30, parked the car, and Amayi immediately went to go help with the cooking. I tried, but they wouldn’t let me help. Mtemba found some kids and instantly was off to play. Tim and I wandered around, watching the preparations and celebration. Soon, it was time to eat. They had made TONS of food. Amayi said that women from Good Samaritan had been up at Thandie’s house for days, prepping and cooking. There was a delicious line of food. They had rice, nsima, three different types of meat, beans, cabbage, a carrot/green bean/bell pepper salad, and a cucumber/carrot/potato/green pepper salad, and then fresh bananas for dessert. We fixed our plates and sat down to eat. We happened to be at the table where the cooked chicken for a ceremony later was being kept. We had to keep Chiboda’s hands off it-he wanted the whole platter.

After we finished eating, more people were still arriving and getting in line to eat. A large group of kids from Good Samaritan were able to come. There were several hundred people by the time everyone had finished eating, including a large group of Ngoni, dressed in a traditional costume. Mother Thandie specifically came and got Mtemba so she could introduce him. She even gave him his own Ngoni headdress to wear. He was SO excited. Justie and Nathan were there too! It was nice not being the only Azungu, but we still stuck out like sore thumbs.

Eventually, the dancing started. Groups were called up to dance, and celebrate the engagement. Tim even went up, and danced with a group of Good Samaritan kids. I was too chicken to go.

The announcer began to call up different groups to show support for the couple. Everyone danced their way up, and pulled out huge stacks of small bills that they threw into a huge basket one by one. It was a lot of fun. This went on for a couple of hours, until it began pouring. It was raining really hard and everyone ran for cover. Some people dove under tables, others stood with their backs pressed up against the house under the eaves, some people made it inside the house, and a few people (Chiboda included here) had brought huge umbrellas, and had people huddling around them. Tim? Nope. He was already soaked, so his argument was that he can’t really get more wet than he already was, so he gave up his umbrella space to a couple kids from Good Samaritan.

All through the rain, the Ngoni dancers continued on with their dance. They didn’t look phased at all about the fact that they were getting soaked-they just kept on dancing as if nothing was wrong. Through all of this, we still had not seen Peace, or her fiancee Geoffrey. The rain eventually stopped, everyone came out from hiding, and the dancing resumed.

There were many different ceremonies that symbolized the two families coming together. First was the exchanging of the chickens. Elders from both Peace and her fiancee’s families came forward with a live chicken. After some speeches and verbal commitments to support their new family member, Klaus (Peace’s uncle) and a man from Geoffery’s family exchanged chickens. This not only strengthens the breeding stock of their respective flocks, but also symbolizes that the families are in a way trading Peace and Geoffrey, and just as the family will care for this new chicken, they will also care for and support their new daughter/son -in-law.

Another of the ceremonies involved a cooked chicken. Klaus and the groom’s uncle both held on to different sides of a whole roasted chicken. They basically played tug-of-war with it, like we do with the turkey wishbone at Thanksgiving. Little surprise here, Klaus won. They then took their respective pieces of chicken and tore off pieces, distributing the meat to various family members, per tradition.

After another downpour, and more dancing, the next ceremony began. Peace, as well as several of her friends, wore a zitenje over their heads and shoulders, so we couldn’t tell who was who. Her fiancee had to pick her out of the lineup. Luckily, he succeeded.

More singing and dancing and giving of money happened, while Peace went back inside. Eventually, she came out again, with a full Ngoni escort, in a beautiful champagne colored dress. This dress was picked out by Thandie, and she wore it as a symbol that she is part of her family. Some ceremonial speeches were given, and she was escorted back inside.

While she was inside, the Ngoni warriors had a symbolic fight with her fiancee. Since he is not Ngoni, he had to earn the right to marry Peace by combat. He had to prove that if someone were to try to come for Peace, he could protect her and their family. He singlehandedly beat 5-6 Ngoni warriors, and was declared to be honorary Ngoni now. In Malawian custom, the man leaves his village/family and joins his wife’s village. He earned the right to be called Ngoni and join the family. They gifted him with an Ngoni headdress, club, and shield.

After a while, Peace returned, with her full Ngoni escort. This time, she was in a beautiful crimson dress that matched her fiancee’s outfit. This was a dress that he chose for her, and it symbolized her becoming part of his family now.

After more dancing and giving money to the couple, a huge line of women formed, with wedding gifts on their heads. They were receiving everything from sleeping mats, to hoes, a machete, baskets, all sorts of things. Amayi and I jumped in the back of the line with our gifts–a set of tupperwares and a laundry basket. We danced our way up to the front to deliver them. On our way, women came and ‘purchased’ the gifts from us, and took them. They delivered the gifts, and Amayi and I took the money up to the big money basket.

With all of the dancing that had been going on all day, and the rain, the yard was no longer grass, but a giant mud pit. Amayi and I were filthy by the time we got back, and many women had given up and taken their shoes off. We went to wash our feet off and try to salvage our shoes, before getting ready to head out. We played a game of life-sized Rush Hour trying to get the car out, but eventually made it. I thought I was going to crash into another car at one point because we were sliding in the mud caused by all the traffic, but I managed to keep the car under control. We got to watch the sunset as we headed back to town.
