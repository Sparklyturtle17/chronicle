Mdunga Village            
                 
       

This is another guest post by Mom.  Again, I wanted to share more details than a 9 year old would be likely to give.

We had the special opportunity to go visit Mdunga Village that is being supported by a new nonprofit organization, Alinafe Global Outreach (AGO) www.alinafeglobaloutreach.org  The organization is run by a good friend of ours named Emmanuel. We would have not survived this trip to Malawi without him and his lovely family.  He founded AGO in October, in honor of his late father, a pastor who devoted his life to the care and support of orphans, agogos, and single women in his village.    We prepared sacks of essentials for the agogos and school supplies for the children, and on Monday afternoon, we flew up to Lilongwe.  When we landed, we met Janet, the Country Director for AGO, and a childhood friend of Emmanuel.  She was so nice and is going to be our guide and translator for our trip out to Kasungu.  We got a car, and drove out to a really nice hotel.      

The next morning, we met up with Janet and hit the road.  We drove for about an hour and a half on the paved road, before turning off onto a dirt road.  We had been told it was about 2 hours from the hotel.  They were wrong.  We had another 2.5 hours on the dirt road before arriving at Emmanuel and Janet’s village.       We were the very first white people MOST of these people had ever seen.  Janet told us that the majority of the people in this remote village wouldn’t ever even have the opportunity to travel the 3 hours into town, much less, make it to Lilongwe or some of the more developed areas. We stopped for a quick hello to Janet’s mother, because she hadn’t gotten to visit in a while.  It was clear how proud she is of Janet.  Her graduation picture is proudly hung on the wall in the main room.         Then we headed to Emmanuel’s mother’s house.  We pulled up to his mother’s house and a huge group of women began singing and dancing in greeting for us.  It was really cool.  We jumped in and danced along with them.  I also carried a 30kg sack of rice back to the kitchen on my head and the women were super impressed-even if I had 2 hands on the sack.  I got to see what they were all cooking and it smelled DELICIOUS.  They were also surprised that I knew what kholowa was, and was excited to eat it.    

After the women finished singing and dancing, we went to tour the local clinic.  I can’t call that place a hospital-it was so sad.  We visited the maternity ward.     They had 3 beds and 4 mattresses.  The other women were sleeping on reed mats on the ground.     In Malawi, the maternal mortality rate is insanely high, so the president has made the decision to require women to go live at the hospital from 32 weeks onward, so they can have better access to care.   This way, if the baby comes early, or if she lives far away, she can be at the hospital to receive care and assistance for delivery, rather than on the road to the hospital.  I remember how we had a car and a hospital nearby, and Olivia was still almost born outside the hospital.  So many of these women/babies don’t even have a chance.  The delivery room consisted of 2 beds with dingy mattresses, a curtain between the two beds, and a bucket of water ‘for hygiene’.  It was wild.  They had no supplies—nothing that you see in even the most basic clinics at home.  Nothing electronic-no monitors, no oxygen machines, nothing.  We didn’t see any bandages or stethoscopes or anything of that sort either.  The beds didn’t even have sheets on them.    

Meanwhile, in the more easily accessible/developed/wealthy areas, there are much nicer 'maternity waiting villages' and much better access to healthcare.  This design provides for both the physical and mental health of expectant mothers, by giving them both that communal environment that they are used to living in, while also giving privacy and enough space/ventilation to help prevent the spread of disease.  Here is a link to learn more about this design, I thought it was really interesting.  https://modelofarchitecture.org/projects/maternity-waiting-village  

The head nurse spoke for a bit about the struggles they face, namely the fact that their water pump had broken and they are at the top of a mountain.  The pregnant women have to hike down the mountain to fetch water every day.

After we toured the hospital, we went to visit one of the orphans that AGO supports.  The  definition of ‘orphan’ in Malawi is different that what I was used to.  This young man lost his father about 4 years ago, but still lives with his mother.  When his father passed, they were kicked out of their house, so they are now living in a different tiny house.  This house wasn’t even made of bricks.  The walls were made of the reed mats that they bring out for people to sit on when there are visitors.  This young man is in Standard 8–he will be beginning secondary school in the fall.  He says that mathematics is his best subject, and he wants to be a bank manager when he grows up.  He was very quiet and shy, but very thankful for the small gift of school supplies that we brought him.  His mother talked about how it is hard for her to provide for him, and pay his school fees because she has no skills or training to be able to produce any income.  Without AGO, he wouldn’t have clothes, a backpack, or the fees for school.  For Janet’s birthday a few weeks back, she asked that instead of gifts, people donate pairs of shoes for the orphans.  He was lucky enough to receive a pair of those shoes.  

After we finished with him, we went to visit an old agogo out in his home.  We drove as far as the road could take us, then we parked the car and we thought we had arrived.  We followed Janet and a couple other members of the AGO team to a collection of houses.  We walked past all of the houses, through a soccer field, and started down a big hill.  It wasn’t a hill.  It was a mountain.  We had a 15 minute hike down the mountain to reach his home.     This old man was so sweet.  His vision faded because of diabetes and for the past 7 years, he has been completely blind.  His son lives with him and cares for him.  He was so happy and thankful that azungu came to visit him, since he couldn’t go up to the church where the event is going to be.  He was so appreciative as we named off for him each of the items that we brought him.  He was especially excited about the new golf shirt (polo).  He showed off the holes in the old shirt that he was wearing, excited that he wouldn’t have to wear it anymore.  GoGo especially liked talking to him.       

After we hiked back up the mountain to the car, we headed back to Emmanuel’s mother’s house for lunch.  Alinafe Global Outreach provided lunch for all of the groups they support—the women’s groups, the agogos, and the orphans. They were surprised that we all ate nsima, and didn’t just take rice.  Everyone ate and then we walked over to the church for the event.  

The women’s groups danced and sang some more for us.  It was really cool.  Janet translated the gist of their songs for us.  One was welcoming the azungu to their home, one was saying that they will all pray for AGO, and one was a fun song talking about how awesome Emmanuel is—he was able to rise above the poverty in his village, and ‘make it’ in the world, and then he chose to come back home and help all of them.  ‘Emmanuel is a machine-i’   There were many speeches about AGO and the work that they do.  The Traditional Authority (similar to mayor) was there and very appreciative of the gifts that we brought to help everyone.  They even had each of us get up and speak.  When speaking to the group, we specifically tried to point out how great the agogos are, and how they are our ancestors and deserve our respect and compassion, not abuse.  In Malawi, many people, especially in the more rural areas, believe in witchcraft and accuse the agogos of being witches.  Many elderly people are abused both verbally and physically, and have no one to stand up for them.  We tried to point out that these people are our parents and grandparents. We are taught to respect our elders—at what point does a beloved grandmother suddenly turn into a witch?  Mtemba even asked to speak, and addressed the children.  He said that he too, was an orphan, and that ‘if we all focus on school and improving our lives, we can change the world.’  It was really sweet.     After speeches, we passed out the gifts.                 The agogos were SO happy.  It was really cute to see.   The children, on the other hand, were definitely afraid of us.  Some didn’t even want to approach to receive their gifts and only warmed up enough to come get them once they realized there were sweets inside.  

Once the gifts had all been passed out, the singing and dancing began again in ernest.  The agogos were SO thankful for their gifts and wanted to celebrate.  We had fun singing and dancing with them.       So many of them wanted to come up and thank us, shake our hands, and give us huge hugs. The children finally warmed up to us, and Olivia had a huge group crowded around her singing different children’s songs with her.               Matemba had a whole crowd of kids sitting around him.  He was making them die of laughter.  He was just making silly faces and noises, and had them captivated.   Eventually, we had to say our goodbyes and head out.  We had a long drive back to the hotel.  We got back about 10 minutes before the restaurant closed for the evening, and thankfully, they still served us.  Then, we crawled in our beds, exhausted.
