Alinafe Kapsyepsye Inaugural Visit            
                 

This is a guest post from Mom about the great work that Alinafe Global Outreach has begun in Kapsyepsye Village.

          On Saturday, Amayi and I went out to her village to meet with the women that have been selected for the first womens' group for Alinafe Kapsyepsye!  We had a wonderful time.  First, when we arrived and got everything set up, we welcomed the women to Alinafe and Amayi explained about Alinafe.  She told them the history of the organization-starting with Pastor Rex Tipote Mbewe, Emmanuel's father.  She explained how Emmanuel was raised in a village just like theirs in Kasungu, and now works to help alleviate poverty in rural Malawian villages.  The women were very excited.  Amayi then explained that they had been chosen to be part of Alinafe Kapsyepsye's first women's group.  They were so excited that we had a uniform for them!  Local village banks typically each choose a uniform every year, and it is a matter of pride for the women--that they are members of this bank.  Most of these women have never had the money to be able to participate in a group that had a uniform.  They were singing and jumped up to dance in celebration.  They were so appreciative over something so simple--a $3.00 piece of fabric.          

After we passed out the uniforms, we divided the women into four different groups.  We had four different activities arranged, that they rotated through.  The first rotation was to get to know the people in their group.  While each of these women were all from the same Village, they live in different villages and don't interact too often.  This is something that was very confusing for me at first, let me explain: A Village (Kapsyepsye Village, or Mdunga Village, for example) is run/managed by a chief.  The chief for Kapsyepsye is lovely--she is very kind and was so grateful to have the opportunity to have Alinafe come into Kapsyepsye and help empower her people.  The chief is in charge of a number of villages (in Kapsyepsye, there are 8).  The smaller villages are more like neighborhoods.  These are made up of a whole group of relatives.  Amayi's village consists of her grandmother and her grandmother's sister's descendants. They all live within a short walking distance and are all related through maternal bloodlines.  I think of these as villages with a lowercase v, while the big Village, Kapsyepsye, has a capital V. This isn't official or anything, it is just how I wrap my mind around it.  So we chose 4 women from each village, or family group, to make 32 women.  When we divided the groups, I was very specific to choose one woman from each village, so they were all mixed up.  This will help them make friends outside of their family, and also will help when the women are trying to sell their products.  If two women in the same village are selling the same thing, neither will be as successful.

The second activity was to take short video interviews of each of the women.  They stated their name, and described their current living situation.  They discussed their daily struggles and worries.  Some of their stories are so sad.  I can't wait to see how Alinafe is able to help them change their lives for the better.

The third activity was to have a small snack of juice and bread.  It is important to me to feed the women whenever I can.  Today, we had so much to do, so it was only a snack, but they were so appreciative.  I was happy to see that when they were mixing up the juice (from concentrate) they specifically left a good portion of the concentrate to give to the chief.  They weren't greedy or selfish-they each took their fair share and left plenty for others.  People might think the reason these women are the lowest in their villages is because they are bad people, or had made poor choices to get them that far down on their luck.  It really isn't true.  Even among these people who are basically starving, they were respectful and grateful.

The last activity we had for them was to learn a craft.  Amayi and I came up with items that could be produced using cheap, locally available materials, and sold.  The problem with these women isn't that they are lazy, or stupid.  They simply don't have the skills necessary to produce things to sell, or the capital required to get up and started with a business.  We provided both of those things for them.  Each of the four groups learned a different craft that they could produce to sell.  Fabric covered notebooks, wristlets for your keys, braided mats (think placemats, table runners, coasters, trivets--that sort of thing), and greeting cards.  Each woman walked through the steps, with Amayi and I correcting and guiding them the whole way.  They were then given materials to complete a few more items on their own.  They will bring those as 'homework' when we meet next.  The looks of pride when they completed their projects were unforgettable.  The joy in their smiles when they came to me for approval of their work was priceless.  I am so blessed to be able to be a part of their journeys.

   

After we finished the four rotations, we came together again as a group.  The women were so happy and excited.  Amayi spoke with them some more, explaining that they will meet regularly.  We set our next meeting, and got permission from the headmaster of the primary school to use a classroom in the afternoons, after the younger students have gone home for the day.  As we discussed the plans, the women kept interrupting with singing.  These poor women who had nothing going for them in life, finally have hope.  They finally are being told that they are loved, and worthy of that love.  They will be pushed aside no longer
