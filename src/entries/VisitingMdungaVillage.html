This is another guest post by Mom. Also, I want to take a minute to acknowledge another guest who has been in the past couple of posts. Walrus, the Penguin. Ms. Rae has a children's travel blog where Walrus the Penguin, aka the World Waddler, travels the world. He explores all sorts of cool places, and Ms. Rae documents all of the places he visits. He was a great addition to our trip, and is in several of our photos.

Sunday morning, we woke up at 5:30 to grab breakfast, and headed out to Mdunga Village. Janet took us by a different route, so the drive wasn't nearly as long and painful as the last time we were out here. First, we stopped at the home of the sweet agogo who we hadn't had time to see the last time we were here. She was so excited that we had finally come. She was dancing and was so happy that azungu had come and were inside HER home. We sat inside her house and talked for a while. We didn't notice at first, but Lorissa was practically sitting on a broody hen. The poor hen started fussing so we finally scooted over to give ehr some room. The agogo was happy to share her story with us, and was thrilled with the nice warm blanket that we gave her. As we were leaving, the agogo gave us a bunch of zimbe. Rae took a ton of pictures of the children. They LOVED it. They were all so happy and shouting Malawi!!!

Second, we stopped at an orphan's home. He lives with his grandparents because both of his parents have passed away. He wants to be a bank manager when he grows up. I guess that is a prestigious and popular job out here in the village, because the orphan that we met last time also wanted to be a bank manager. It was interesting to learn about his home.

Next, we went to Janet's mother's house. We got to see Janet's mother again, and we met one of her brothers. We were supposed to drop off Shama, but he wanted to come with us, to play with Matemba.

After loading back in the car, we headed to Emmanuel's mother's home. When we arrived, the women's group was there singing and dancing to welcome us. This time, I was a lot more familiar with things, and so I jumped right in the dancing. The women looked great in their uniform, and it was fun to get to be a part of that. I really need to learn the songs, so I can sing along with them next time. Rissa and Aimee got convinced to dance as well, while Rae started snapping photos again. She was really popular with the children, but also got some great family shots of women with their children.

Next, we loaded up a mattress into the back of the car, and crammed Emmanuel's mother in with us, to head out to the church where she preaches. We hadn't arrived in time for the service, but everyone was still there waiting to greet us. The children's choir sang for us, as the adults were inside singing. Their little shelter has a cemented floor, but the walls and roof were thatched. They are hoping to raise enough money to build a proper building to worship in, but I am afraid it will take a long time-they dont have much to spare. They were happy to meet us, and sat us in a place of honor-on the only chairs in the building, up in the front. Rae was able to get a few smiles from them as we heard about their church.

Finally, we went to my favorite stop! We visited the blind agogo who we had gone to see last time. He was so happy to see us again. Janet explained that I was the only returning visitor--that Gogo and Aunt Olivia had returned back to America. Agogo was very concerned and wanted to make sure that they had arrived safely. I assured him that they were both home safe and sound, and that Gogo was back to taking care of her chickens. He thought that was really funny. When we presented him with the gifts we had brought, he was so excited. We brought him a thick blanket, and a mattress. He said that he was going to be sleeping like a 'soft child' now. That is what they call the rich kids in Malawi. His family then explained to us that Agogo had always called a mattress 'the place where azungu sleep'. He was so surprised to have a mattress of his own. It reminded me of when Amayi took a wooden carved pipe out to her father. He was shocked that it was for him, because "only azungu could afford those things." It is so sad to me that such basic comforts are so unattainable for people out in the villages. The pipe cost me $4.28, and the mattress was only $29.71. I will gladly give up a single meal out at a restaurant, to give people such 'luxuries'.

After hiking back to the car, we headed to the hospital. The women's group was there waiting for us, dancing and singing, to celebrate the hospital beds and mattresses that Alinafe was delivering for them. Also, the technicians were there working on replacing the water pump! They were so appreciative. After so long with no water, the village leaders and the hospital staff had had a meeting and came to the terrible conclusion that they were going to have to permanently close the hospital. The water situation was just not sustainable and without water, the hospital is unhygienic, and unsafe. Alinafe heard about this, and fortunately, were able to scrounge up the money to save the hospital. One of the members of the local team for Alinafe had just had a baby--his wife had just given birth in the hospital, and we got to see the new baby. His name is Famous. He was adorable. I am so thankful that his birth was uneventful, adn that Mom and baby are both doing well, despite the lack of water. As the repairmen were working on the pump, they discovered bees in the tower for the water tank. That was an experience, but hey-they got to have a snack of wild honeycomb afterwards. It turned out to be much more complicated than they had anticipated, and there was still no water by the time we left.

After leaving the hospital, we headed back to Emmanuel's mother's house for lunch. We had a fantastic meal of nsima, rice, local chicken, and cassava leaves. Then, we got to work. All of the agogos had assembled and we passed out blankets and shoes to them all. They were so excited and thankful. Janet was speaking to them, and they were all able to point me out as being a returning visitor, and they all were drawing checkmarks in the air for me--checking off that Alinafe was doing a good job. I thought it was really sweet to see all of these elderly women (and one elderly gentleman today) so enthusiastic and approving of what Alinafe is doing in their community.

After that was finished, Lorissa and Janet started sorting and passing out clothing to the orphans, while Aimee and I went over to do some demonstrations for the women's group. I showed them a few simple crafts that they could make to generate some income. They were really excited, and thankful that Alinafe was going to provide them all with some starter supplies for them to work with.

After all of that, we found Rae printing tons of photos for all of the children. She printed as many as she could, and they loved it. While we were doing everything, Janet received a call from the hospital. The water is finally working! I know that so many peoples' lives will be changed for the better because on one small thing--a water pump that is actually pretty similar to the well Gogo has in her garage at home. The entire thing--including the pump itself, delivery all the way out to the village, bricks and cement, and the 2 days of installation (with bee attacks) only cost about $1,800.

We finally loaded up in the fortuner and headed back to the hotel. By the time we got back, it was late and I was exhausted. Mtemba wasn't even interested in dinner, so he and I went straight to bed, while Aimee, Rissa, Rae, Janet, and Shama went down to dinner.

It was such a busy day, but I had a lot of fun. I love seeing the impact we are able to make on people. These single women, who struggle to survive in the village and feed their children, have really turned their lives around with the help of Alinafe Global Outreach. Alinafe has given them a sense of purpose and I hope that with the job/skill training that they are providing, and the startup capital that they are trying to provide for everyone, these women can really improve their lives. Instead of being the bottom tier of impoverished women in the village, they are going to rise up to the top. I can't wait to see that happen.
